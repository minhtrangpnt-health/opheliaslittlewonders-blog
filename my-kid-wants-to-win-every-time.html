For parents

What if my kid wants to win every time?

Your living room does not need a permanent champion.

Kid: “I win.”
Grown-up: “We have not started.”
Kid: “I am getting ready.”

Set the rules before the game

Wanting to win and changing the rules halfway through are different things.

  • You can welcome excitement while keeping an ordinary agreement about how everyone will play.

Raising Children Network recommends making fair play more important than the result.

  • It also suggests reminding children that games are for enjoyment, rather than treating winning as the only worthwhile ending.

Before you begin, try, “Everyone gets one turn. At the end, someone might win and someone might feel disappointed.”

  • Keep the explanation short enough that you can remember it yourself when the dice go missing.

Choose a fair contest

The network notes that a game of chance can give children a more equal opportunity against older players.

  • That may suit a family with very different ages better than a test of speed or advanced skill.

You could also choose something cooperative, simply as an alternative family activity: invent a story together or build an imaginary city.

  • There does not have to be a winner in everything you do.

If your child asks you to lose on purpose, talk about what sort of game they want.

  • “Would you like a pretend game where you choose the story, or this game with the usual rules?”
  • Both can have a place if everyone knows which one they are playing.

Make room for other players

Winning can be enjoyable without teasing someone who lost.

  • You might model, “That was a fun round.
  • Thanks for playing with me.”
  • Avoid making your child's win a comparison with a sibling's ability.

If rules are repeatedly ignored, stop the round calmly.

  • “We agreed on one turn each.
  • We can pause now and decide later whether to try again.”
  • There is no need to threaten that nobody will ever play with them again.

Later, ask which part was hard: waiting, uncertainty, losing, or someone else's celebration.

  • Choose one thing to practise rather than delivering a speech about character.
  • The next game can be short enough to try that single skill.

Game: a different celebration

Invent three ways to finish a pretend game: a quiet bow, a friendly wave, or saying, “Thanks for playing.”

  • Let your child choose one for the whole family to try.

Practise as both winner and loser.

  • Nobody has to hug or shake hands if they would rather wave.
  • The celebration can include everyone who played, without changing who won.

Sources: Raising Children Network, “Playing fair: tips for children and parents”.
