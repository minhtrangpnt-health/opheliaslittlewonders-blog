For parents

Why are my kid’s feelings so big after school?

The school bag comes off. Everything else comes out.

Grown-up: “How was school?”
Kid: “Do not ask questions.”
Kid, a moment later: “Why are you not talking?”

Begin with what you can notice

The teacher reports a settled day, but your child is crying before the shoes are off. You do not have to choose which version is “real”. Start with the child who is here now.

Norfolk's children's health service suggests giving children space to recover after school and adjusting demands to what they can manage.

  • Raising Children Network also identifies hunger, tiredness and overstimulation as possible contributors to difficult emotions.

That is a reason to check the basics, rather than announce a diagnosis or decide your child has been saving difficult behaviour especially for you.

  • You might simply say, “That was a big day.”

Make the landing quieter

Try offering an ordinary snack, somewhere comfortable and a little less conversation.

  • “I am glad to see you.
  • We can talk later.”
  • Keep your first request small: perhaps shoes in one spot, with the rest waiting.

You can make a practical choice about the afternoon.

  • Does the extra errand have to happen today?
  • Could another adult collect a sibling while you stay home?
  • There is no perfect after-school schedule to copy.

If siblings need different things, name that calmly.

  • “You want to tell me your story.
  • Your brother wants quiet.
  • Let us find room for both.”
  • You can listen to one child without requiring the other to join in.

Talk when there is room

Later, offer a specific opening instead of a whole-day report. “Was there a part you wish had gone differently?” Let your child choose whether now is a good time.

If a pattern keeps appearing, share observations with the teacher: when it happens, what your child says and what seems to help.

  • Ask about friendships, transitions and the ordinary shape of the day without assuming a particular cause.

You might say to your child, “I will help work out what is hard. We do not have to solve it all this afternoon.”

  • If they mention something unsafe, take that seriously rather than treating every upset as tiredness.

Try this: choose your landing

Offer two simple arrivals: sit together quietly, or do a slow walk around the room.

  • Your child can point instead of explain.
  • Give the choice a silly name if they enjoy that: “Sofa landing or snail landing?”

Try it yourself too. This is a small option for today, not a rule that everyone has to follow after every school day.

Good to know: If distress keeps affecting school or home life, talk with the teacher and your GP or child health nurse.

Your turn

What does your child seem to need before the first after-school question?

Sources: Norfolk and Waveney Children and Young People's Health Services, “Meltdowns and Distressed Behaviour”; Raising Children Network, “Tantrums: why they happen and how to respond”.
