For parents

How can I help when my kid feels left out?

“Nobody played with me” deserves a pause in the grown-up day.

Grown-up: “Could I join your game?”
Kid: “You can be the tree.”
Grown-up: “A very important role.”

Listen before recruiting friends

Your child says they were left out.

  • Before arranging a playdate or sending a message, ask what happened in their own words.
  • Was it one game, one child, or something that keeps happening?

Raising Children Network explains that friendships support belonging and that a strong relationship at home provides a steady base through friendship difficulties.

  • You can offer that base before deciding the next step.

Try, “That sounds lonely. Tell me about the part when you wanted to join.”

  • Avoid promising that everyone will want to play every time.
  • You can take hurt seriously without making a promise about other children.

Practise a way in

The network suggests giving children opportunities to practise social skills and supporting playdates when appropriate.

  • You might begin with something smaller: rehearse one invitation together in a calm moment.

Ask, “What were they playing? Was there a job you could ask to do?”

  • Possible words include, “Can I help build?”
  • or “Is there room for another customer?”
  • Treat these as options, not a script that guarantees entry.

You can also ask whom your child enjoys spending time with elsewhere.

  • A neighbour, cousin or friend from another activity can be part of their social world.
  • One difficult group does not need to become the whole story about friendship.

Notice repeated exclusion

If your child describes an ongoing pattern, contact the teacher with specific observations.

  • Ask what staff have noticed and how they can support safe, inclusive play.
  • Avoid asking your child to handle repeated mistreatment alone.

Keep the conversation with your child open: “I will talk with your teacher about helping. You can keep telling me what is happening.”

  • Explain the plan instead of secretly organising every part of their social life.

After a better day, listen to that too.

  • You do not have to question whether yesterday's sadness was genuine.
  • Different days can contain different experiences, and your child can tell you about both.

Game: an invitation with a space

Pretend to run a tiny restaurant with no equipment. One person is the cook and one is a customer. Practise asking to join, then invent a job for the newcomer.

Swap once.

  • If your child wants to be the tree outside the restaurant, that is allowed too.
  • End by asking which invitation felt most like something they would actually say.

Your turn

Who helps your child feel included, inside or outside school?

Sources: Raising Children Network, “School-age friendships: how to support them”.
